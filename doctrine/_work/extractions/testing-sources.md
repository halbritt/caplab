## Per-theme evidence notes

### Suite economics and the four-pillar frame (chapters 1, 4)

Chapter 1 grounds the whole book: tests are code, code is a liability, and a test stays in the suite only while its value clearly exceeds its recurring upkeep costs (refactoring alongside the code, execution on every change, false-alarm triage, reading) — extracted as `testing-suite-net-value` with the successful-suite properties from section 1.4. Section 1.3 (spanning flattened 1.3.1-1.3.4, including the 100%-coverage-mandate war story) supplies the coverage material: line and branch coverage prove only execution, never verification, and mandated coverage targets create a perverse incentive — routed as SRC-UT support extending `testing-coverage-is-not-adequacy` and as the nomination for `prohibit-coverage-number-target`. Chapter 4 supplies the book's declared frame of reference (`testing-four-pillar-test-value`): four attributes whose multiplied scores give a test's value, mutual exclusivity of the first three by extremes, and resistance to refactoring as the non-negotiable attribute; its false-positive analysis (implementation-detail coupling, trust erosion, lifecycle dependence in section 4.2) is extracted as `testing-assert-observable-outcomes`, and the Test Pyramid material in section 4.5 (flattened 4.5.1-4.5.2) as `testing-test-level-mix` plus the black-box-workflow refinement of `testing-coverage-is-not-adequacy`.

### Definition, schools, and anatomy (chapters 2, 3)

Chapter 2 documents the classical-versus-London split on what "isolated" means (London take in flattened 2.1.1, classical in 2.1.2, dependency-taxonomy sidebars embedded under section 2.1). The classical resolution — isolate tests from each other, not units from collaborators; substitute only shared or slow out-of-process dependencies — is extracted as `testing-shared-dependency-substitution` (confidence contextual: the argument presumes a codebase one is free to design). Unit-of-behavior versus unit-of-code (2.3.1, with the naming corollary from 3.4) becomes `testing-unit-of-behavior`; the large-interconnected-graph argument in section 2.3 (load-bearing material in flattened 2.3.2 and 2.3.4) becomes `testing-testability-as-design-signal`. The school split itself is preserved as `conflict-classical-vs-london-test-isolation`, anchored in chapter 5 where the fragility payoff lives. Chapter 3 is a self-described refresher and earns one structural record, `testing-self-contained-test-anatomy` (single AAA sequence, no branching, fixture reuse via private factory methods from section 3.3); its multi-line-act-phase observation (flattened 3.1.4) is routed as a contextual refinement of `universal-explicit-invariants`.

### Mocks, styles, and refactoring toward value (chapters 5-7)

Chapter 5 supplies the observable-behavior/implementation-detail criterion (an operation or state serves an immediate client goal, section 5.2) routed as a refinement of `implementation-risk-driven-tests`, and the fragility argument (sections 5.3, 5.4) anchoring the preserved school conflict; 5.4.1 establishes that a fully application-controlled out-of-process dependency is part of the application, the precursor rule for `testing-mock-unmanaged-dependencies-only`. Chapter 6 ranks the three unit-test styles on refactoring resistance and maintainability (output > state > communication) with the functional-core/mutable-shell mechanism (section 6.3) and the adoption gate of section 6.5 — extracted as `testing-output-based-style-preference`. Chapter 7 contributes the two-axis code classification and Humble Object split (`testing-humble-object-testability-split`, with the testability/controller-simplicity/performance trilemma from section 7.4) and the domain-significant-precondition rule (flattened 7.3.3 inside section 7.3) refining `implementation-risk-driven-tests`.

### Integration, mocking practice, and the database (chapters 8-10)

Chapter 8 defines the integration-test budget (edge cases in unit tests, one longest-happy-path integration test per business scenario, Fail Fast as a documented alternative — `testing-integration-scope-selection`), the managed/unmanaged dependency distinction with the mock-only-unmanaged rule (section 8.2, with the no-tests-if-no-real-database corollary in flattened 8.2.3 — `testing-mock-unmanaged-dependencies-only`), the support-versus-diagnostic logging rule (section 8.6, folded into the same record), and the single-implementation-interface YAGNI argument (section 8.4, with the in-process extension in embedded 8.4.3) refining `architecture-dependency-inversion-gate`. Chapter 9 directs mocks at the outermost owned adapter with literal-message assertions and independent spy checkpoints (`testing-mock-at-system-edges`; edge-precision proportionality caveat in embedded 9.1.3). Chapter 10 supplies the delivery prerequisites (schema and reference data in source control, migration-based delivery, per-developer instances — `testing-database-delivery-prerequisites`) and production-fidelity rules (same DBMS vendor, per-section units of work, sequential execution, cleanup at test start — `testing-database-production-fidelity`); the asymmetric write/read threshold (10.5.1) refines `implementation-risk-driven-tests`. Section 10.4's craft material (Object Mother, decorator act methods, fluent assertion helpers) is noted but below doctrine threshold.

### Anti-patterns (chapter 11)

Chapter 11's anti-patterns consolidate into one record, `testing-no-test-privileged-access`: tests interact with the system under test exactly as production clients do — no publicized private methods or state (11.1, 11.2, with the rare ORM-contract exception), no reimplemented algorithms in tests (11.3, which also endorses legacy output as an expected-value oracle when refactoring), no test-only production switches (11.4), no partial mocks of concrete classes (11.5), and time injected explicitly rather than read from ambient context (11.6). Each cited section carries its own narrowly scoped contribution.

### Preserved disagreement and legacy-doctrine tensions

The book advocates the classical school; the London school's one-class-per-test granularity and mock-all-collaborators isolation is documented fairly in chapters 2 and 5 and preserved as `conflict-classical-vs-london-test-isolation` with WELC's seam pragmatics recorded as the legacy-context variant of the substitute-freely position. Khorikov's rules repeatedly assume a designable codebase with a controllable environment; WELC assumes protection is otherwise impossible. These tensions are routed through the concepts' conflicts lists (`conflict-classical-vs-london-test-isolation`, `conflict-temporary-seam-vs-long-term-design`, `conflict-broad-vs-narrow-characterization`) rather than silently merged.

xUnit/NUnit framework mechanics and parameterized-test syntax (chapter 3, sections 3.2, 3.5, 3.6); the CRM walkthrough particulars and CanExecute/Execute plus domain-event mechanics beyond the trilemma rule (chapter 7); integration-test layering corroboration in section 8.5 (as few indirection layers as possible, eliminate circular dependencies) — flagged as corroborating `universal-earned-abstraction` and `architecture-earned-boundary` but not cited, to respect selectivity; test-code reuse craft in section 10.4; and the CQS mocks-versus-stubs taxonomy of section 5.1 beyond what the derived records need.

### Source limitations carried into the corpus map

Published 2020, single author; examples are C#/.NET-centric (xUnit, Moq, Entity Framework) though the rules are argued language-independently. The evidence base is practitioner reasoning over enterprise-application experience — argued from first principles but not controlled or comparative, and scoped to enterprise-style applications with domain models, databases, and external services. The classical-school advocacy is an argued position within a live community disagreement, not neutral ground. Numerical illustrations (coverage thresholds, pyramid ratios) are consequence illustrations, not portable gates. Single-source support caps every derived record at strong or contextual confidence.
